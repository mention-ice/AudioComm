Acoustic modem v6

Data over sound: one device plays a frame through its speaker, and every device in the room decodes it through its microphone, with FSK, OFDM or a single carrier with SMAP detection.

How it works

A frame has two parts. The control part is sent at 400 bit/s, at 4 / 8 kHz or hopping, two modes every receiver knows in advance: 0.1 s warm-up · MLS preamble · header (OFDM and the single carrier have a shorter one by default since v6: see below). The message part uses the tones and rate chosen on the transmitter: coded and scrambled message · MLS postamble (the same sequence as the preamble). The header carries the type (text, image or room sounding) and size of the message, the code, the rate and the tones (for OFDM, the guard and the mapping; for the single carrier, the mapping), protected by a CRC-8 and the convolutional code, so the receivers need no settings, as with the SIGNAL field of Wi-Fi. Bit 0 is a burst of the low tone, bit 1 a burst of the high tone; with 4 tones, each burst carries 2 bits (see below). audiocomm_v6.py produces and decodes the same frames.

The receivers find a frame by correlating what they hear with the three possible preambles (maximum-length sequences of 127, 255 or 511 bits). A longer preamble gathers more energy before deciding that a frame starts: each doubling of its length gives about 3 dB of margin, so phones farther away, or in a noisier room, still find the frame; the header is then sent 2 or 4 times so that it is read as far away. Neither helps once the message itself is too weak: for that, also lower the rate (200 bit/s gives the message 3 dB more) and use a code. The preamble also gives the instant of every symbol. Its long symbols (2.5 ms) tolerate an error of several samples, but the message's short ones do not (0.6 ms at 1600 bit/s). So with tone hopping, once the message is in, the receivers fine-tune its timing on the message itself, by up to half a symbol or 0.5 ms, and keep it if it makes the two tones of each symbol clearly more distinct.

The receivers read the code from the header, so only the transmitter chooses it. Hamming (7,4): 3 parity bits per 4 bits, corrects 1 wrong bit in each block of 7 (hard decisions). Convolutional: rate 1/2, constraint length 7 (generators 171, 133 octal), decoded by the Viterbi algorithm on the soft outputs. Turbo: rate 1/2 as in 4G: two small recursive convolutional codes, the second fed with the message shuffled, decoded by two soft decoders that exchange what they learnt, up to 8 times. It needs about 1.3 dB less signal than the convolutional code for the same frame error rate. Hamming, convolutional and turbo bits go through an interleaver, which spreads a burst of errors (an echo, a cough) over many code blocks. v0 also offered Reed–Solomon (255,223), which corrects up to 16 wrong bytes in each block of 255. In a room it fails as early as Hamming, since both decide each bit before decoding, so this page no longer sends it; it still decodes it.

Tone hopping. In a room, the sound of each bit rings on for a large part of a second, far longer than the bit (2.5 ms at 400 bit/s). With only two tones, the echo of the earlier bits sits on both tones all the time; beyond the critical distance, where the echo is louder than the direct sound, it buries the bit. Hopping spreads the bits over 6400 / R pairs of adjacent tones from 3.2 to 16 kHz (16 pairs at 400 bit/s, R the rate): bit k uses pair 7k mod 16 (s k mod H in general), so a pair is used again only H bits later and the echo on each tone is H/2 times weaker, about 3 dB per doubling of the pairs. The preamble and header hop too (at 400 bit/s), otherwise far receivers would not find the frame. Each tone has its own level (speaker, room, microphone), so the receiver levels the tones before deciding. The band starts at 3.2 kHz: below that, a small speaker gives little and a room is noisy, and in a real test the 1.6 kHz tone the band first started at caused nearly all the errors.

Four tones (v2). With 4 tones, a symbol carries 2 bits, Gray-coded so that neighbouring tones differ in one bit only. At the same bit rate it lasts twice as long and gathers twice the energy, and its tones, spaced by the symbol rate (half the bit rate), take the same band as the 2 tones of binary FSK. The receiver then compares 4 energies instead of 2, which non-coherent detection turns into a gain: in simulation, about 2 dB in noise and 2 to 3 dB in a room's echo, or roughly 25 to 40 % more distance, and 3200 bit/s becomes usable, where binary FSK would make errors even close to the speaker. Longer symbols also tolerate twice the timing error. The fixed tones are 4, 5.6, 7.2 and 8.8 kHz (or 8 to 12.8 kHz); with hopping, the 6400 / R pairs become groups of 4 adjacent tones. The control part stays binary, so every receiver finds the frame and reads the header; receivers older than v2 find no 4-tone mode in it and ignore the frame. With 4 tones the header's 4 rates are 400 to 3200 bit/s, and the postamble and the message are each padded to a whole number of symbols.

6400 bit/s. At 6400 bit/s, with 4 tones, a symbol lasts 0.31 ms (15 samples at 48 kHz) and its tones, spaced 3.2 kHz, are 3.2, 6.4, 9.6 and 12.8 kHz: one group fills the whole band, so there is nothing left to hop over, and the echo of the earlier symbols always lies on the same 4 tones. FSK can go no faster in this band, because sending one tone at a time carries at most half a bit per second per hertz; going further takes many tones at once, each with its own phase (OFDM, as in Wi-Fi: see below). In simulation it needs about 4.7 dB more SNR and 3.6 dB more DRR than 3200 bit/s, so it reaches 60 to 70 % of that distance. Choosing 6400 selects Hopping, the only tone set that spans the band. The header marks it as tone set 3, which receivers made before it ignore.

OFDM (v3). FSK sends one tone at a time; OFDM sends hundreds at once, each with its own phase. An inverse FFT of N samples at 48 kHz turns one complex number per subcarrier (k × 48000 / N Hz, from 3.2 to 16 kHz) into a symbol: with the long guard, N = 2048, 546 subcarriers 23.4 Hz apart and a symbol of 42.7 ms. Before each symbol its last 21.3 ms are sent again, the cyclic prefix or guard interval. An echo that arrives within the guard then only changes the amplitude and the phase of each subcarrier (the room's frequency response) instead of spilling into the next symbol; only the echo that arrives later acts as noise. Each subcarrier carries 2 bits per symbol as a phase step from the symbol before (DQPSK: π/4, 3π/4, 5π/4 or 7π/4, Gray-coded), so the receiver compares each symbol with the previous one and needs no estimate of the room's response. The frame is the hopping control part, then a known reference symbol, the data symbols, and the reference symbol again. On the two reference symbols the receiver finds where to place its FFT windows (it plots the impulse response they see, with the guard) and the time scale: a clock offset, or a phone moving at 0.1 m/s (300 ppm), turns the phase between them in proportion to frequency, and the receiver undoes it by resampling. The rms is set to half the peak (peaks above are clipped), so OFDM plays 3 dB quieter than FSK. With the long guard: 17.1 kbit/s before the code, 8.5 kbit/s with turbo 1/2, 4.3 kbit/s with turbo 1/4. In simulation, where echo limits the link, OFDM with turbo 1/4 (4.3 kbit/s) reaches at least as far as FSK at 6400 bit/s (3.2 kbit/s), and nearly as far as FSK at 3200 bit/s when most of the echo arrives within the guard; where noise limits the link, it needs about 2.5 dB more signal than FSK at 6400 bit/s. The short guard (N = 1024, 5.3 ms) is 20 % faster, but in a reverberant room much of the echo arrives after it: compare both to see why the guard must outlast the room's echo. Receivers older than v3 ignore OFDM frames.

Coherent OFDM (v4). DQPSK compares each symbol with the one before, so the receiver needs to know nothing about the room, but it pays for it: the comparison doubles the noise, and a phase step carries 2 bits at most in practice. With pilots, the reference symbol's values sent on 1 subcarrier in 12 of every data symbol (shifted by 3 from one symbol to the next: 8 % of the subcarriers), the receiver measures the gain and the phase of every subcarrier, the room's frequency response H, and decides on each symbol by itself: Y = H X + noise, so X ≈ Y / H. Then QPSK (2 bits per subcarrier) needs about 2 dB less signal than DQPSK, 8PSK carries 3 bits, 16QAM 4, 64QAM 6 and 256QAM 8, their amplitude carrying bits too, which is why the gain must be known as well as the phase. The estimate of H takes the two reference symbols and every pilot, removes from each symbol its own small change (a gain, a phase and a delay: a phone that moves by a few millimetres between symbols turns the high subcarriers by tens of degrees), averages, and smooths across the band, since an echo that arrives within the guard can only make a response that varies slowly from one subcarrier to the next. It costs about 1.5 dB against a receiver that would know H exactly. In simulation, with turbo 1/2 and the long guard: DQPSK 8.5 kbit/s; QPSK 7.8 kbit/s with 2 dB to spare in noise and 1 dB in echo; 8PSK 11.7 kbit/s for about 1 dB more than DQPSK; 16QAM 15.6 kbit/s for about 3 dB more (4.5 dB in a room whose echo is all diffuse), roughly 70 % of the distance; 64QAM 23.5 kbit/s for about 5 dB more than 16QAM in noise (1 to 4 dB in echo); 256QAM 31.3 kbit/s for about 11 dB more than 16QAM in noise (2 to 9.5 dB in echo), a third of 16QAM's distance where noise limits the link. 256QAM's points are so close that clipping the loudest peaks of the signal already flips about 5 % of its bits, which the code corrects. The receiver plots the received points divided by H: a tight cloud around each point means a clean channel, a cloud that spreads into its neighbours means errors for the code to correct. The header marks the mapping by xoring its CRC with a mask, so receivers older than v4 ignore these frames. v4 also follows, for DQPSK, the delay change from one symbol to the next: with v3, a phone swinging by 2 cm lost one frame in five.

Short control part (v6). At 400 bit/s the control part takes 0.65 s, longer than the whole OFDM part of a short text. OFDM and single-carrier frames therefore start, by default, with a control part of 0.19 s sent like OFDM, over the whole band at once. Its preamble P is one symbol of 2048 samples (42.7 ms) on the 546 subcarriers from 3.2 to 16 kHz, all of the same amplitude, sent three times in a row; its phases are those of OFDM's reference symbol negated, so that the two sweep the band in opposite directions and are not mistaken for each other. Then, after a guard of 21 ms, one symbol H carries the same 92 coded header bits as the 400 bit/s header, one bit per subcarrier as a sign (BPSK): subcarrier j carries bit 37 j mod 92, so that each bit sits on 5 or 6 subcarriers spread over the band. The receiver passes what it hears through a matched filter of P and adds up the three periods: in noise the sum is about 1/1100 of the energy heard (at most 0.012 over our recordings), while a frame gives the share of its energy that its strongest path carries (0.17 to 0.35 in an ordinary room, 0.05 in a very reverberant one), and a frame is declared above 0.02 if the last two periods agree. The last two periods, which the room has filled, give its frequency response, the clock offset between them, and the noise on each subcarrier. The message part starts from the first strong arrival of that response, as the single carrier's timing does, and H is read against the response, the echo that arrives after its guard counted as noise. On two recordings of the v6 test sound in a reverberant room (Impasse Carnot), all 16 control parts, short and long, were found and read. Near the speaker the short one survived 7.5 dB less added noise than the 400 bit/s one (3 dB of it because it plays as loud as OFDM, rms 0.45, against FSK's 0.64), still noise 50 dB above the room's own; farther away both survived the same noise, the echo costing the 400 bit/s tones 9 dB and the short control part about 1, and there the messages failed long before either control part. Receivers older than v6 do not read it: the long control part is kept for them, and for the longest range. FSK frames and the room sounding keep the 400 bit/s control part.

Single carrier with SMAP (v5). OFDM pays for its simple receiver twice: the guard (a third of the time with the long guard) and its peaks, since hundreds of subcarriers add up to a signal whose rare peaks must be clipped. The single carrier sends one complex symbol at a time instead, 9600 per second on a carrier at 9.6 kHz, each shaped by a root-raised-cosine pulse (roll-off 1/3) so that the signal fills the same band, 3.2 to 16 kHz, with no guard. The room then smears every symbol over the next few hundred (intersymbol interference): the receiver writes what it hears, at the symbol rate, as y = c ∗ s + noise, with c the room seen through the two pulse filters (256 to 1024 taps, plotted below the received image). It learns c from known training symbols (QPSK, at the start and then every 0.5 s, which also give the clock and follow the room as people move), and then looks for the symbols. The training plays the role of OFDM's guard: the longer it is, the longer the echo the receiver can learn, for some of the rate. Short training (640 symbols) learns echoes up to 20 ms after the first strong arrival (256 taps), medium (1280) up to 47 ms (512 taps), long (2560) up to 100 ms (1024 taps, kept when they fit the room better than 512). A dry room needs only the short one; in a reverberant room, where the echo 50 ms on is still 15 to 20 dB down, the long one decodes 256QAM where the others fail. A linear MMSE equalizer undoes c as well as the noise allows, but it treats the symbols as Gaussian. SMAP (Ramos, Senecal and Yang) uses what the receiver knows: each symbol lies on the constellation. It minimizes |y − c ∗ s|²/N₀ − Σ ln p(s), with p a mixture of narrow Gaussians around the constellation's levels, by gradient steps that need only FFTs, and gives each bit a soft output. The detector and the turbo decoder then exchange their beliefs on the coded bits a few times (turbo equalization): the decoder's beliefs sharpen p, which sharpens the detector's output, until the decisions form a codeword. Choose the detector on the receiver, and its effort: fast stops SMAP after 30 steps (20 on later passes) and turbo equalization after 4 passes, full allows 150 (100) steps and 8 passes. Fast cost at most 1 dB at the threshold on our recordings, and takes a third to two thirds of the time (a phone may need it). Either switch decodes the last frame again. On two recordings at one spot, the single carrier with SMAP needed 4 to 6 dB less SNR than OFDM at 256QAM (17 to 19 dB against 22 to 23) and 2 dB less at 64QAM, for 2 to 5 times OFDM's computation (more near the threshold, where it takes several passes). The decoding runs in the background (a few seconds, more for large images), so the receiver keeps listening. The header marks single-carrier frames with a rate field that older receivers do not know, so receivers older than v5 ignore them.

Everything else comes with each frame, in its header. Soft SMAP is the detector of v5; linear MMSE, the classical equalizer, is there to compare (it needs about 3 to 4 dB more signal at 256QAM). What these numbers mean
Soft output (LLR)

Each receiver gives the decoder a log-likelihood ratio per bit, LLR = ln P(what was heard | bit 1) / P(what was heard | bit 0): its sign is the decision, its size the confidence. The soft decoders (Viterbi, turbo) need both, on the right scale. The histogram under the results shows the LLRs of a frame, split by the bit sent.

FSK: the calibrated LLR measures, on the known bits of the postamble (sent at the message's tones and rate), the level A and the noise σ² of each tone, then uses the log-likelihood ratio of non-coherent FSK, ln I₀(A₁E₁/σ₁²) − A₁²/2σ₁² − ln I₀(A₀E₀/σ₀²) + A₀²/2σ₀². If the postamble is not found (for example when the end of the sound was cut off), A and σ² are estimated on the message itself: its bits are guessed from the energies, then the guesses and the estimates are refined in turn (EM). With the postamble, the LLR is then multiplied by the factor α ≤ 1 that best predicts the postamble's own bits: the echoes of short symbols have heavier tails than Gaussian noise, which makes the model too sure of itself (α ≈ 0.3 at 1600 bit/s in a real room). It corrects an imbalance between the tones (speaker, microphone, room) and gives the decoder reliabilities on the right scale. With 4 tones, each tone m gets its own λm = ln I₀(AmEm/σm²) − Am²/2σm², the log-likelihood that it was the tone sent, and the LLR of each of the symbol's two bits is ln Σ eλ over the two tones where that bit is 1, minus the same over the two where it is 0. The energies are first divided by the local signal level (averaged over ±24 symbols), because in a room the main disturbance, the echo, rises and falls with the signal.

Earlier versions also offered the plain ratio ln(E₁/E₀) of the two tones' energies. Its sign is the right decision when both tones arrive equally strong, but its size says little about reliability: a bit drowned in noise can look as sure as a clean one. Since v1 also calibrates when the postamble is lost, the calibrated LLR has done better (on the 1600 bit/s recording where the ratio once won: 0 against 256 wrong pixels), so the page no longer offers the ratio.

OFDM uses the LLR of its detector. DQPSK: for each subcarrier, z = Yi Yi−1* compares the symbol with the one before, the LLR of the first bit is −√2 Re z / N0 and that of the second −√2 Im z / N0, and N0 (noise plus the echo that arrives after the guard) is measured on the decisions, per group of 32 subcarriers. QPSK to 256QAM (v4): that of the estimated channel, for each bit ln Σs: bit 1 exp(−|Y − H s|² / N0) − ln Σs: bit 0 exp(−|Y − H s|² / N0) over the points s of the mapping, with N0 measured on the pilots and the reference symbols.

Single carrier (v5): the detector (soft SMAP or linear MMSE) gives the LLRs; the turbo decoder returns what it learnt about every coded bit (its extrinsic LLRs), and the detector starts again with that as prior, pass after pass (turbo equalization). The histogram shows the detector's first pass.

Did the decoding succeed? With a turbo code, the receiver checks whether the decoder's decisions on every coded bit, message and parity alike, agree with the re-encoded message, that is, whether they form a codeword. If they do, the message is almost surely right (✓ decoded); if not, some bits are wrong (✗). With the demo image, which every receiver knows, it also counts the wrong bits. Hamming and the convolutional code give no such check.

Room sounding

Choose Sound the room on the transmitter. After the usual control part (preamble and header, so that the receivers know what follows) it plays a maximum-length sequence of 2n−1 samples of ±1 at 48 kHz, several times in a row. The circular autocorrelation of an MLS is a single spike, so correlating one received period with the sequence gives the impulse response h(t) of the whole chain: speaker, room and microphone. Averaging the periods lowers the noise; the first period is skipped, so that the room already rings in its steady state.

From h(t): the reverberation time RT60, the time the sound energy takes to fall by 60 dB, from the slope of the Schroeder energy decay curve (the backward integral of h²) between −5 and −25 dB (T20) or −15 dB (T10); the direct-to-reverberant ratio (energy within 2.5 ms of the direct sound against the rest); C50 (first 50 ms against the rest); the rms delay spread τrms, to compare with the duration of a bit (2.5 ms at 400 bit/s): echoes much longer than a bit cause intersymbol interference. The frequency response shows how strongly each tone arrives, and the noise heard just before the frame gives the SNR per frequency, turned into the Es/N0 a tone of the modem would get.

These are the parameters of a room model: a direct path, an exponentially decaying diffuse tail with the measured RT60, per-frequency gains and noise. audiocomm_v6.py sim plays any frame through a measured impulse response (downloaded above) to try the modem offline in that room.
